# Today's News Roundup: Iran, Anthropic, Markets, Floods and a New King in Norway

## This Day in History: August 28 Has a Habit of Making History

Some dates seem to collect history. August 28 is one of them.

On August 28, 1963, roughly 250,000 people gathered in Washington, D.C., for the **March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom**. The demonstration brought together civil rights, labor, religious and political organizations demanding federal action on racial equality and economic opportunity. The event culminated at the Lincoln Memorial, where Martin Luther King Jr. delivered his famous “I Have a Dream” speech. The National Archives describes the march as one of the largest demonstrations for human rights in American history and notes that its organizers met with President John F. Kennedy afterward. ([National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/official-program-for-the-march-on-washington?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Official Program for the March on Washington (1963) | National Archives"))

King's speech became much larger than the event itself. The Library of Congress has recognized the address as a landmark of the civil rights movement, and the recording now sits in the National Recording Registry. What makes August 28 particularly interesting in 2026 is that history is once again colliding with the present: thousands are gathering in Washington today for a new **March on Washington** focused on voting rights and civil rights. ([Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/static/programs/national-recording-preservation-board/documents/IHaveADream.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com "I Have a Dream speech"))

Five years later, on August 28, 1968, Chicago became the scene of another politically charged confrontation. Protesters gathered around the Democratic National Convention, and clashes with police in Grant Park were broadcast around the country. The violence became one of the defining images of a deeply divided America during the Vietnam era. ([HISTORY](https://www.history.com/articles/yippies-1968-dnc-convention?utm_source=chatgpt.com "How the Yippies 'Stuck It to the Man' at the 1968 DNC | HISTORY"))

That makes today's date unusually relevant. August 28 connects one of the most celebrated demonstrations for American civil rights with one of the most infamous confrontations between protesters and authorities. And in 2026, the country is again arguing about voting, government power and the boundaries of political protest.

History doesn't repeat itself neatly. It does, however, occasionally pick up the phone and call.

## Climate, Disasters & Environment

**Nepal and China face a catastrophic flood disaster:** Rescue operations resumed in the Himalayas after devastating flash floods near the Nepal-China border. The Associated Press reports that more than 500 people have died and more than 1,500 remain missing, while Reuters reports that rescue efforts were briefly suspended because of the danger posed by an upstream lake created after a glacier collapse. ([AP News](https://apnews.com/article/nepal-tibet-china-glacier-floods-ae9973b5fd2b1dc6d54c3e88b1b55bdc?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Death toll rises in Nepal-China border disaster as a lake poses new flood threat"))

The disaster illustrates how difficult emergency response becomes when geography, extreme weather and rapidly changing mountain conditions collide. More than 90,000 people are estimated to have been directly affected by the wider landslide disaster, according to the Red Cross. ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/environment/over-90000-affected-by-himalayan-landslide-red-cross-says-2026-08-28/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Over 90,000 affected by Himalayan landslide, Red Cross says"))

**A stronger El Niño is raising another climate warning:** A new study reported by the Associated Press suggests that El Niño events have become substantially stronger since the pre-industrial era. Researchers used coral records from the Galápagos to reconstruct centuries of ocean temperatures, while acknowledging that some scientists question aspects of the findings. ([AP News](https://apnews.com/article/cf2e0bbe4b241c5ad0141ca0f93f44dd?utm_source=chatgpt.com "As super El Nino strengthens, study suggests that climate change is intensifying El Ninos"))

That matters because El Niño can influence weather patterns across enormous portions of the planet. Stronger events can mean greater risks of flooding, drought, heat and other extreme conditions.

## Technology, AI & Digital Life

**A federal judge rules against the Pentagon in the Anthropic fight:** A U.S. judge has ruled that the Pentagon's decision to designate AI company Anthropic as a "supply-chain risk" was unlawful. The dispute began after Anthropic refused to accept unrestricted military use of its AI systems, particularly for autonomous weapons and domestic surveillance. ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/us-judge-rules-pentagon-blacklisting-anthropic-unlawful-2026-08-28/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "US judge rules Pentagon blacklisting of Anthropic unlawful"))

The ruling is bigger than one company. It raises questions about how much control the government should have over technology companies that supply increasingly powerful AI systems — and whether a company can establish ethical limits on how its technology is used by the military.

It is also an unusually direct collision between national security, artificial intelligence, corporate contracts and constitutional rights.

**Tech companies are warning that AI could make hacking worse:** More than 100 technology and financial companies have signed a joint letter calling for a stronger defensive response to AI-driven cyberattacks. The companies argue that increasingly capable AI could make sophisticated hacking more accessible and more scalable. ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/major-tech-companies-call-defensive-surge-defeat-ai-driven-hacks-2026-08-27/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Major tech companies call for defensive surge to defeat AI-driven hacks"))

This is one of those stories that sounds abstract until it isn't. AI can help write software, analyze information and automate work. The same capabilities can also be applied to finding vulnerabilities and attacking computer systems.

The technological race is therefore becoming a race between AI-powered offense and AI-powered defense.

Oil remains one of the clearest economic links between the Middle East conflict and everyday life elsewhere. ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/oil-track-weekly-loss-even-iran-tensions-simmer-2026-08-28/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Oil drifts lower as Hormuz flows balance slow progress in Iran diplomacy"))

## Health & Science

**Ebola continues spreading in eastern Congo:** The Ebola outbreak in eastern Democratic Republic of Congo has expanded into additional health zones, with authorities reporting thousands of confirmed cases and deaths. The outbreak involves the Bundibugyo strain, for which there is currently no approved vaccine. ([AP News](https://apnews.com/article/615dcbccd5b37f68aca89f8e286f4d7b?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Ebola outbreak in eastern Congo spreads to 2 new health zones with a total of 60 areas now affected"))

Health authorities are dealing with more than the virus itself. Insecurity, displacement, mobility and difficulty reaching communities are complicating efforts to contain the outbreak.

The story is a reminder that infectious disease outbreaks are rarely purely medical problems. Public trust, transportation, politics and security can all determine how quickly an outbreak spreads.

## The Bigger Picture

What stands out about today's news is how often the stories are really about **control**.

Who controls a war once it stops going according to plan? Iran and the United States are still trying to shape the outcome of a conflict that has already lasted far longer than originally expected.

Who controls elections? That question is being argued in American courts while people march in Washington on the anniversary of one of the most important civil rights demonstrations in U.S. history.

Who controls artificial intelligence? The Anthropic case puts that question into a courtroom, where the government and a technology company are arguing about military use, corporate independence and constitutional rights.

And who controls the economy? That question will be partly answered by what the Federal Reserve decides to do about inflation and interest rates.

Even the disaster stories fit the pattern. Governments and rescue organizations are struggling to control events in an environment where weather, geography and technology can move faster than institutions can respond.

Perhaps that is the larger story today. Modern life increasingly involves systems that are bigger, faster and more interconnected than the people trying to manage them.

And every once in a while, a date like August 28 reminds us that we've been arguing about power and control for a very long time.
